Module 04

Understanding Bilirubin Metabolism

schedule 15 min read

Bilirubin is a yellowish pigment that is made during the normal breakdown of red blood cells. Bilirubin passes through the liver and is eventually excreted out of the body. Understanding this pathway is crucial for diagnosing various hepatic and hematologic conditions.

info Key Concept

The liver is responsible for conjugating bilirubin, making it water-soluble so it can be excreted in bile. Elevated levels of unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin typically indicate pre-hepatic issues, while elevated conjugated (direct) bilirubin suggests hepatic or post-hepatic issues.

The Pathway of Breakdown

When senescent red blood cells are destroyed by macrophages, predominantly in the spleen, hemoglobin is broken down into heme and globin. The heme ring is then opened by heme oxygenase to form biliverdin, which is subsequently reduced by biliverdin reductase to yield unconjugated bilirubin.

Hepatic Processing

Unconjugated bilirubin is lipophilic and must be transported in the blood bound to albumin. Upon reaching the liver, it dissociates from albumin and enters hepatocytes. Inside the hepatocyte, it is conjugated with glucuronic acid by the enzyme UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1), rendering it water-soluble.

medical_services Clinical Note

Gilbert's Syndrome is a common, benign, hereditary condition characterized by mildly elevated levels of unconjugated bilirubin. It is caused by a genetic mutation that leads to reduced activity of the UGT1A1 enzyme.

Following conjugation, direct bilirubin is secreted into the bile canaliculi, flows through the biliary tree, and enters the intestine. In the intestine, bacterial flora reduce it to urobilinogen, which is either excreted in feces as stercobilin (giving stool its characteristic brown color) or reabsorbed and excreted by the kidneys as urobilin (giving urine its yellow color).
